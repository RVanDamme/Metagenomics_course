Introduction

Microbiome used

In this tutorial we will compare samples from the Pig Gut Microbiome to samples from the Human Gut Microbiome. Below you’ll find a brief description of the two projects:

The Pig Microbiome:

Pig is a main species for livestock and biomedicine. The pig genome sequence was recently reported. To boost research, we established a catalogue of the genes of the gut microbiome based on faecal samples of 287 pigs from France, Denmark and China. More than 7.6 million non-redundant genes representing 719 metagenomic species were identified by deep metagenome sequencing, highlighting more similarities with the human than with the mouse catalogue. The pig and human catalogues share only 12.6 and 9.3 % of their genes, respectively, but 70 and 95% of their functional pathways. The pig gut microbiota is influenced by gender, age and breed. Analysis of the prevalence of antibiotics resistance genes (ARGs) reflected antibiotics supplementation in each farm system, and revealed that non-antibiotics-fed animals still harbour ARGs. The pig catalogue creates a resource for whole metagenomics-based studies, highly valuable for research in biomedicine and for sustainable knowledge-based pig farming

The Human Microbiome:

We are facing a global metabolic health crisis provoked by an obesity epidemic. Here we report the human gut microbial composition in a population sample of 123 non-obese and 169 obese Danish individuals. We find two groups of individuals that differ by the number of gut microbial genes and thus gut bacterial richness. They harbour known and previously unknown bacterial species at different proportions; individuals with a low bacterial richness (23% of the population) are characterized by more marked overall adiposity, insulin resistance and dyslipidaemia and a more pronounced inflammatory phenotype when compared with high bacterial richness individuals. The obese individuals among the former also gain more weight over time. Only a few bacterial species are sufficient to distinguish between individuals with high and low bacterial richness, and even between lean and obese. Our classifications based on variation in the gut microbiome identify subsets of individuals in the general white adult population who may be at increased risk of progressing to adiposity-associated co-morbidities

Whole Metagenome Sequencing

Whole Metagenome sequencing (WMS), or shotgun metagenome sequencing, is a relatively new and powerful sequencing approach that provides insight into community biodiversity and function. On the contrary of Metabarcoding, where only a specific region of the bacterial community (the 16s rRNA) is sequenced, WMS aims at sequencing all the genomic material present in the environment.

The choice of shotgun or 16S approaches is usually dictated by the nature of the studies being conducted. For instance, 16S is well suited for analysis of large number of samples, i.e., multiple patients, longitudinal studies, etc. but offers limited taxonomical and functional resolution. WMS is generally more expensive but offers increased resolution, and allows the discovery of viruses as well as other mobile genetic elements.

Prepare and organise your working directory

All the work of this tutorial is done on your own computer, in the folder ~/mag2026/d1. Every command below is run from this folder, unless the text says otherwise.

mkdir -p ~/mag2026/d1
cd ~/mag2026/d1

If you close your terminal and come back later, activate the tools again (see the top of this page) and return to the folder with cd ~/mag2026/d1.

Getting the Data and Checking their Quality

As the data were very big, we have performed a downsampling on all 6 datasets (3 pigs and 3 humans). We will first download and unpack the data.

cd ~/mag2026/d1/data
curl -O -J -L https://osf.io/h9x6e/download
tar xvf subset_wms.tar.gz
cd ~/mag2026/d1

The reads are now in data/sub_100000: two files per sample (_1.fastq and _2.fastq), and a metadata.txt file.

We’ll use FastQC to check the quality of our data.

mkdir -p results/fastqc
fastqc --outdir results/fastqc data/sub_100000/*.fastq

FastQC writes one .html report per file in results/fastqc. Open a few of them in your web browser.

TipQuestion

Compare a pig sample (ERR1135...) with a human sample (ERR3215...). Look at the graph “Per base sequence quality”. What differs?

Trimming and filtering with fastp

FastQC only reports the quality. fastp cleans the reads: it removes adapter sequences, trims bad ends and drops reads of low quality. It keeps the two reads of a pair together.

mkdir -p results/fastp
for i in data/sub_100000/*_1.fastq
do
    prefix=$(basename $i _1.fastq)
    echo $prefix
    fastp --in1 data/sub_100000/${prefix}_1.fastq --in2 data/sub_100000/${prefix}_2.fastq \
        --out1 results/fastp/${prefix}_1.fastq.gz --out2 results/fastp/${prefix}_2.fastq.gz \
        --detect_adapter_for_pe --thread 2 \
        --json results/fastp/${prefix}.fastp.json --html results/fastp/${prefix}.fastp.html
done

For each sample, fastp prints a summary and writes a report (.html) in results/fastp.

With 6 samples and 2 tools we already have 18 reports. MultiQC reads them all and makes one summary report:

multiqc --outdir results/multiqc results/fastqc results/fastp

Open results/multiqc/multiqc_report.html in your web browser.

TipQuestion

How many reads did fastp remove in each sample? Which samples lost the most reads, and why were these reads removed?

TipQuestion

The pig samples and the human samples come from two different studies, sequenced in different places at different times. How could that explain what you see? Why does it matter when you compare the two groups?

Note

Reads deposited in public archives are sometimes already cleaned, sometimes not. Never assume it: always check the quality of the data yourself.

From now on we work with the cleaned reads of results/fastp.

Taxonomic Classification

Kraken 2 is a system for assigning taxonomic labels to short DNA sequences (i.e. reads). It looks up the k-mers of each read in a database of reference genomes and assigns the read to the lowest taxon that explains them. It is extremely fast compared to traditional approaches (i.e. BLAST).

Note

Older versions of this tutorial used Kraken 1 with the minikraken database from 2017. Kraken 1 is no longer supported by its authors, so we use Kraken 2. The principle is the same.

The result of a classifier depends on its database. The standard Kraken 2 database contains the RefSeq genomes of Archaea, Bacteria, viruses and plasmids, plus the human genome and common vectors. The full database is far too large for a laptop, so we use Standard-8: the same content, reduced to fit in 8 GB. Fewer reads are classified than with the full database. Always report which database, and which version, you used.

The Kraken 2 authors provide ready-made databases on the index page. The download is 5.9 GB. If your teacher gives you a copy on a drive, use that instead of downloading.

# First we create a databases directory in the course folder
mkdir -p ~/mag2026/databases/k2_standard_08gb
cd ~/mag2026/databases/k2_standard_08gb
# Then we download the Standard-8 database (version of 22 October 2026)
curl -O https://genome-idx.s3.amazonaws.com/kraken/k2_standard_08_GB_20261022.tar.gz
# check that the download is complete: the result must be 7447d230ea402d0b523c14d217b5c20b
md5sum k2_standard_08_GB_20261022.tar.gz
tar xzf k2_standard_08_GB_20261022.tar.gz
rm k2_standard_08_GB_20261022.tar.gz
KRAKEN_DB=~/mag2026/databases/k2_standard_08gb
cd ~/mag2026/d1
Warning

Keep the database inside your Linux home (~), not on the Windows drive (/mnt/c/...): reading it from there is very slow.

Now run Kraken 2 on the reads

for i in data/sub_100000/*_1.fastq
do
    prefix=$(basename $i _1.fastq)
    # print which sample is being processed
    echo $prefix
    kraken2 --db $KRAKEN_DB --memory-mapping --threads 2 --paired \
        --output results/${prefix}.tab \
        --report results/${prefix}_tax.txt \
        results/fastp/${prefix}_1.fastq.gz results/fastp/${prefix}_2.fastq.gz
done
Note

--memory-mapping tells Kraken 2 to read the database from the disk instead of loading all of it into memory. The database file is 8 GB, more than the memory of most laptops, so without this option Kraken 2 stops with an error or is killed. The first sample is slower than the next ones.

For each sample this produces two files. The .tab file has one line per read pair, with the TaxID assigned to it. The _tax.txt file is the report: a “tree” view with the percentage of reads assigned to each taxon. Take a look at the _tax.txt files!

TipQuestion

Which percentage of the reads was classified in each sample? Is it the same for the pig and the human samples?

Abundance estimation with Bracken

Look at one report: many reads are assigned to a genus, a family or even higher, because their k-mers are shared by several species. So the report underestimates the species. Bracken uses the Kraken 2 report and the database to re-distribute these reads to the species level, and gives one estimated number of reads per species.

for i in results/*_tax.txt
do
    prefix=$(basename $i _tax.txt)
    bracken -d $KRAKEN_DB -i results/${prefix}_tax.txt \
        -o results/${prefix}.bracken -w results/${prefix}.bracken_report.txt \
        -r 100 -l S -t 10
done
  • -r 100: the read length. Our reads are about 90 bases long, and 100 is the closest value the database offers.
  • -l S: estimate at the species level.
  • -t 10: ignore species with fewer than 10 reads, which are mostly noise.
NoteOn macOS

Bracken is in its own environment on macOS (see Setup). Open a second terminal and run:

cd ~/mag2026/Metagenomics_course
pixi shell -e bracken-mac
cd ~/mag2026/d1
KRAKEN_DB=~/mag2026/databases/k2_standard_08gb

Then run the loop above in that terminal.

Each .bracken file is a table with one line per species. Look at the most abundant species of a pig sample:

sort -t$'\t' -k7,7gr results/ERR1135369.bracken | head -5 | cut -f1,6,7

The columns shown are the species, new_est_reads (estimated number of read pairs) and fraction_total_reads.

TipQuestion

Take the first species of the list. According to Bracken, which fraction of the reads does it represent? Now compute it yourself: divide new_est_reads by the number of read pairs that entered Kraken 2 (printed by Kraken 2, and by fastp). Why are the two numbers so different?

TipQuestion

“Species X makes up half of the pig gut microbiome.” Is this sentence supported by the result? Write a sentence that is.

Warning

The fractions of Bracken (and the percentages of most microbiome figures) are relative abundances: fractions of the classified reads, which always sum to about 1. They are not counts of cells. If one species goes up, all the others go down, even if their real numbers did not change.

Host reads

A gut sample also contains DNA of the host (here a pig or a human). Host reads are not part of the microbiome. They waste computing time, they can be assigned to the wrong organism, and human reads are personal data. In a real project, host reads are removed before the classification: the reads are mapped to the genome of the host (for example with Bowtie 2), and only the pairs that do not map are kept.

A host genome index is too large for this tutorial, but we can check how many host reads are in our data. The Kraken 2 database contains the human genome. Its taxonomy identifier (TaxID) is 9606, in the fifth column of the report:

awk -F'\t' '$5 == 9606 {print FILENAME ": " $2 " read pairs (" $1 "% of the sample)"}' results/*_tax.txt
TipQuestion

How many human read pairs are there in the human samples? What does it tell you about the data that were deposited in the public archive?

TipQuestion

The database does not contain the pig genome. If a pig sample contained pig reads, in which line of the report would they be counted? Can this check tell you whether the pig samples are free of host reads?

Note

To practise a real host removal, follow the Galaxy tutorial Remove contamination and host reads (about 1 hour).

Visualization

Krona

Krona draws the taxonomy of a sample as an interactive wheel that you open in your web browser. We first convert each Kraken 2 report to the format of Krona, then make one page for the six samples:

mkdir -p results/krona
for i in results/*_tax.txt
do
    prefix=$(basename $i _tax.txt)
    kreport2krona.py -r $i -o results/krona/${prefix}.txt
done
ktImportText -o results/krona/krona.html results/krona/*.txt

Open results/krona/krona.html in your web browser. On Windows, you can open the current folder in the file explorer with explorer.exe . and then double-click the file. Choose a sample in the list at the top left. Click on a part of the wheel to zoom in.

TipQuestion

Compare a pig sample and a human sample. Which groups are the largest in each? How large is the “Unclassified” part?

TipQuestion

Count the species that Bracken reports in each sample: wc -l results/*.bracken (one line per species, plus a title line). One group of samples seems to have more species than the other. Before you conclude that its microbiome is richer, look again at the number of classified reads in each sample. What else could explain the difference?

Pavian (optional)

Pavian is a web application for exploring metagenomics classification results. It compares several samples in one table and draws flow diagrams. It runs in R (RStudio).

Install and run Pavian from R:

options(repos = c(CRAN = "https://cloud.r-project.org"))
if (!require(remotes)) { install.packages("remotes") }
remotes::install_github("fbreitwieser/pavian")
pavian::runApp(port=5000)

Then upload the _tax.txt reports of the folder ~/mag2026/d1/results and explore the results produced by Kraken 2.

Note

The installation of Pavian downloads several packages from GitHub and can fail when many people do it at the same time from the same network. If it fails, continue with Krona. Pavian is also available on usegalaxy.eu as an interactive tool.

Functional Classification

So far we asked “who is there?”. Shotgun reads can also answer “what can they do?”: the reads are compared with databases of genes and pathways. A widely used tool is HUMAnN (version 3; the older HUMAnN2 is no longer maintained). Its databases are too large for this tutorial. You will meet HUMAnN on Galaxy, in the metatranscriptomics tutorial of Day 3.
